The AI value chain · 2025 figures

Who builds AI, and who gets paid for it

Every chatbot answer runs on a long chain of companies: the makers of chip-printing machines, the chipmakers, the builders of data centers, the clouds, the model labs and the apps. Money enters at the end, from people and businesses, and flows back up the chain.

    Lithography machines, chip designers, foundries and memory makers produce the accelerators that do the math.

Step 1 · Make the chips

How AI chips get made

An AI accelerator passes through more specialist hands than almost any other product. Several of those steps have only one or two companies in the world that can do them.

Design. Nvidia, AMD and the in-house teams at Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta design the chips in software from Synopsys and Cadence. Broadcom and Marvell build custom chips for the clouds.

Manufacturing. TSMC turns those designs into silicon, using extreme-ultraviolet scanners that only ASML builds and tools from Applied Materials, Lam, Tokyo Electron and KLA.

Memory and packaging. Each processor is bonded to stacks of high-bandwidth memory from SK hynix, Samsung or Micron. That packaging step has repeatedly been the bottleneck.

Systems. Taiwanese manufacturers such as Foxconn, Quanta and Wiwynn assemble the chips into servers and full racks, wired together with switches and optics.

China is building its own stack

US export controls cut Chinese companies off from the most advanced chips and chipmaking tools, so a parallel supply chain is growing fast. In 2025 Cambricon's revenue rose 453% to about $910M, Moore Threads grew 243% to about $221M and MetaX grew 121% to about $230M.

Manufacturing is the hard part: SMIC is China's most advanced foundry, and YMTC makes flash memory. Both are still well behind TSMC and the leading memory makers.

Steps 2–3 · Build the data centers, rent the compute

Who builds and runs the data centers

Chips need somewhere to run. An AI campus needs land, hundreds of megawatts of power, liquid cooling and years of construction before it earns anything.

Four kinds of company

Owners and funders

The hyperscalers buy most of the chips and sign the long leases. Their capital spending is the demand signal for everything upstream.

AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Meta, Oracle

Developers and colocation

They secure land and power, finance and build the shells, then lease capacity by the megawatt.

Equinix, Digital Realty, QTS, Vantage, AirTrunk

Builders

General contractors and engineering firms build the halls, power yards and cooling plants.

Turner, HITT, DPR, Holder, Mortenson

Power and cooling

Switchgear, backup power and liquid cooling, now standard for dense AI racks. Lead times run long.

Vertiv, Schneider Electric, Johnson Controls, Delta

How a data center landlord earns

Most of Equinix's money is rent for space and power, billed monthly per cabinet and per kilowatt. The rest comes from fees for connecting tenants directly to clouds and network carriers. About 95% of its revenue recurs.

Beyond the US

Europe is building sovereign clouds that keep data under European law: OVHcloud (€1.08B revenue), STACKIT from the Schwarz Group, Scaleway and Open Telekom Cloud. Nscale reports a contract backlog of up to $14B.

Southeast Asia and Australia are a fast-growing hub, led by AirTrunk, ST Telemedia (a 1.7 GW platform) and Princeton Digital Group, with contractors such as Gamuda building campuses in Malaysia.

Step 2 · Where the compute lives

The world's AI campuses

The largest AI sites draw a gigawatt or more, about the output of a nuclear reactor. They go wherever power, land and permits can be found quickly, which increasingly means rural Texas, the US Midwest, the Nordics and the Gulf.

Why they end up where they do

Power comes first. A 1 GW campus needs a grid connection that can take years to approve, so developers chase places with spare capacity or build their own generation. That's why Abilene, Memphis and rural Louisiana now host some of the biggest sites.

Training can go anywhere; serving users can't. Training a model doesn't depend on distance, so it moves to cheap power in Texas, Norway or Johor. Answering users quickly needs sites near cities, which keeps Northern Virginia, Frankfurt and Singapore busy.

Countries want their own. Governments in Europe, the Gulf, India and Southeast Asia are paying for sovereign compute so their data and AI stay under their own laws.

Steps 4–5 · Train, serve and use models

Models, apps and where the money goes

Model labs are the most visible part of AI and the biggest buyers of compute. Their revenue is growing fast, but it is still small next to what the hardware makers take in.

Growth matters more than the totals. OpenAI ended 2025 at an annual run-rate of about $20B. Anthropic's run-rate rose from $1B in January to $9B in December.

Three ways to make money from models

Sell access

OpenAI, Anthropic and Mistral charge per token through APIs and by subscription through their apps.

OpenAI ≈$13.1B · Anthropic ≈$5.5B · Mistral ≈$200M

Give it away

Meta releases Llama for free and earns nothing directly from it. The models support its ads and apps instead.

Meta: $0 direct model revenue on $201.0B total

Build it into products

Apple, Microsoft and the enterprise software firms add AI to products people already pay for.

Apple: ≈$0.9B from AI software on $416.2B

China's labs earn far less

Chinese labs release strong models, often as open weights, but earn far less from them. Zhipu AI had about $105M of revenue in 2025. DeepSeek's annual recurring revenue was an estimated $13–30M, and Moonshot's 2025 revenue an estimated $15–30M. The largest Chinese AI business is ByteDance, at an estimated $4B annual run-rate.

Step 4 · Serving models

Same model, many hosts, very different prices

Once a model's weights are public, anyone with the hardware can serve it. Dozens of companies do, and they compete on price, speed and privacy. A closed model is sold by its maker and a few cloud partners, so its price barely moves.

How to read this

Same name, different product

Hosts often run an open model at lower numerical precision (fp4 or fp8, where the original is 16-bit) to save memory. That makes it cheaper and can change the answers slightly.

Zero data retention

ZDR means the host says it does not store your prompts or outputs. The flag comes from OpenRouter's list and is the host's own policy, not an audit.

List prices only

These are public prices through OpenRouter. Large customers negotiate their own, and a host's direct price can differ.

Why it matters for the chain

Wide gaps and many sellers are signs of a commodity market. That pushes profit away from serving open models and toward whoever owns scarce chips, power or a model nobody else can sell.

What these charts can't show

Training

No per-token figure here spreads the energy of training over the tokens served. Only Mistral used a life-cycle method, and its per-response number covers inference.

Closed models

No commercial provider publishes energy per token. Google and OpenAI give per-prompt figures without token counts. The third-party values are inferred from API speed and assumed hardware, not measured.

Input versus output tokens

Every per-token value divides a whole query's energy by its output tokens. No source separates the cost of reading the prompt from the cost of writing the answer.

Utilisation and batching

ML.ENERGY measures a 3 to 5 times change in energy per token from batch size alone, and a median 35% reduction moving from H100 to B200 GPUs. A GPU at full load and a half-idle fleet are different quantities.

Cached tokens

No published measurement covers the energy of a cache read. Providers price cache reads at a fraction of fresh input, which is the only available proxy.

Agents

Long tool-using sessions with many short outputs are not represented. Microsoft's long-query estimate of 3.91 Wh is the closest published point.

Humans · Brain-computer interfaces

Brains connecting to machines

A brain-computer interface (BCI) passes information between neurons and a device, in both directions. After more than 50 years of lab research, companies have raised billions of dollars to bring it to patients. So far the devices restore lost abilities, such as letting a paralysed person type by thought.

50+ yearsOf research on humans and animals before today's companies
~38People implanted with Blackrock Neurotech arrays, the most of any company
8.5 yearsLongest-running Utah Array implant
10People with Synchron's Stentrode, several of whom text by thought

Where the electrodes sit

BCIs use electrodes to pick up the electrical activity of neurons. The closer an electrode gets to the neurons, the fewer of them it hears at once and the clearer the signal, but the more surgery it takes to put it there.

Figure 1. A slice through the head, from scalp to brain. Hover or tap a device to see which neurons (triangles) it can hear. A schematic, not to scale.

Spike sorting: telling neurons apart

An electrode in brain tissue hears several neurons at once. Software removes the noise, then uses the height, shape and timing of each voltage spike to work out which neuron fired. Once each neuron's activity is known, it can be matched to an intention, such as moving a cursor and clicking. This step applies to microelectrodes, not to every kind of BCI.

Figure 2. A simulated recording from one electrode near three neurons. The signal is generated for illustration, not recorded from a brain.

Precision costs invasiveness

Figure 6. The trade-off that shapes the industry. Each step toward a clearer signal means more surgery and more cost.

What patients can do today

Text and email by thought

Synchron asks patients to imagine movements, such as a hand moving or clicking, and records the brain activity. It builds a dictionary from activity to intention, so a paralysed person can control a computer again.

Synchron · 10 participants

Control a cursor and design in 3D

Neuralink's first human implant was in 2024. Participants have shown cursor control and 3D object manipulation by thought. The company also aims to restore speech, and sight through its Blindsight project.

Neuralink · at least 7 participants by 2025

Live with an implant for years

Blackrock's Utah Array has the longest record in people, across many research groups. One implant has stayed active for 8.5 years.

Blackrock Neurotech · about 38 participants

Figure 7. Three examples of what implanted systems do for patients today.

What holds it back

The body fights the implant

Protective glia cells wrap around foreign objects they can't break down, including electrodes, and the signal weakens. New materials that mimic brain tissue reduce this reaction.

Precision against lifetime

Fine electrodes that hear single neurons tend to degrade faster than large ones. The draft treats durability past 5 to 10 years as a limit; one reviewer countered that implants have already lasted 7 years or more.

Data, power and heat

More channels mean more data to send, more power to supply and more heat to remove, all inside the skull.

Headsets hear through bone

Non-invasive methods are safer, but the skull weakens and smears the signal, so their resolution is poor.

Reading comes before writing

Most systems today read from the brain. Writing to it, as in vision restoration, is earlier. Non-invasive writing uses magnetic or ultrasound stimulation.

Privacy and agency

Who owns brain data, and who is acting when software sits between intention and action? Regulators have begun to discuss "neurorights".

Where it could go

Today's devices restore what was lost. The same technology could later be used for enhancement: handing arithmetic to a machine, asking an AI a question by thinking it, or receiving sight and sound directly.

These are speculations, not forecasts. They would need fast two-way links with far more bandwidth than exists today, and nobody knows how a brain would adapt to a constant flow of injected information. The article's authors disagree among themselves about how far off this is; one puts direct streaming of senses decades away at least.
